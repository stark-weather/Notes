# Purpose

The purpose of this repository is to document my study of Scripture in a structured and reusable way.

I want to understand more than simply:

> "What does this verse mean to me?"

I want to ask questions such as:

- What did this passage mean to its original audience?
- Who wrote this book?
- When was it written?
- Why was it written?
- What was happening historically at the time?
- What was happening politically around Israel or the early church?
- What cultural practices would the original audience have understood?
- What literary genre is being used?
- How does the surrounding passage affect the meaning?
- How does this passage fit into the entire book?
- How does this book fit into the overall biblical story?
- What does this teach about God?
- What does this teach about humanity?
- What theological ideas appear here?
- Does this passage connect with other parts of Scripture?
- Are important Hebrew or Greek words being used?
- How should this truth affect the way I live?

My goal is to study Scripture carefully before moving to interpretation and application.

# How I Study the Bible

My general study process follows this progression:

```text
Background
    ↓
Context
    ↓
Observation
    ↓
Interpretation
    ↓
Biblical Connections
    ↓
Theology
    ↓
Application
```

The order is intentional.

Application comes **after** understanding what the passage actually says and what it meant within its original context.

---

# Book Overview Studies

Before studying individual chapters of a biblical book, I first create an overview of the entire book.

This provides the larger framework needed to properly understand individual passages.

A book overview may investigate the following areas.

## Basic Information

- Book name
- Testament
- Literary genre
- Approximate date
- Author
- Original audience
- Historical setting

## Authorship

Questions may include:

- Who traditionally wrote the book?
- What internal evidence supports authorship?
- What external historical evidence exists?
- Are there scholarly debates concerning authorship?
- What can be known about the author?

## Historical Background

This section attempts to understand the world surrounding the book.

Questions may include:

- What was happening in Israel?
- Who was ruling at the time?
- What nations surrounded Israel?
- Were there wars occurring?
- Was Israel united, divided, exiled, or restored?
- What empires were dominant?
- What political pressures existed?
- What religious problems existed?
- What social problems existed?
- What events may have caused God to send a prophet?

For New Testament books, this can include:

- Roman rule
- Jewish religious groups
- First-century culture
- Early Christian persecution
- Roman emperors
- Local church problems
- Missionary activity
- Jewish-Gentile relations

## Purpose of the Book

Questions may include:

- Why was this book written?
- What problems was the author addressing?
- What did the original audience need to understand?
- What major message is communicated?

## Major Themes

Themes may include subjects such as:

- Covenant
- Faith
- Grace
- Judgment
- Holiness
- Justice
- Love
- Mercy
- Repentance
- Salvation
- Wisdom
- Worship
- Kingship
- The Kingdom of God

## Structure

I try to understand how the book is organized.

For example:

```text
Genesis

Genesis 1–11
Primeval History

Genesis 12–25
Abraham

Genesis 26–36
Isaac and Jacob

Genesis 37–50
Joseph
```

Understanding the structure helps individual chapters make more sense within the larger argument or narrative.

# Chapter Studies

After studying the book as a whole, I move into individual chapters.

A chapter study focuses closely on the biblical text.

## Chapter Context

Before analyzing individual verses, I consider:

- What happened immediately before this chapter?
- What happens afterward?
- Where are we within the structure of the book?
- Who is speaking?
- Who is being addressed?
- Where is the event taking place?
- What historical situation is occurring?

## Passage Structure

Long chapters are often divided into smaller sections.

For example:

```text
Genesis 1

1:1–2
Introduction

1:3–5
Day One

1:6–8
Day Two

1:9–13
Day Three

...
```

This prevents a long chapter from being treated as one large block of information.

# Observation

Before interpreting a passage, I first observe what is actually present in the text.

Questions may include:

- Who is involved?
- What happens?
- Where does it happen?
- When does it happen?
- What words are repeated?
- Are there commands?
- Are there promises?
- Are there warnings?
- Are there contrasts?
- Are there comparisons?
- Are there cause-and-effect relationships?
- Are there questions and answers?
- Are there changes in tone?
- Does the author emphasize something?

Observation asks:

> **What does the text say?**

---

# Interpretation

Interpretation asks:

> **What does the text mean?**

Questions may include:

- What did this mean to the original audience?
- Why did the author include this?
- How does the surrounding context affect the meaning?
- What cultural information helps explain the passage?
- What historical information matters?
- What literary techniques are being used?
- Is the language literal, poetic, symbolic, prophetic, or figurative?
- How does this section contribute to the author's overall message?

- Prayer

A theme study may include:

What does the theme mean?

## Boundaries

What is it?

What is it **not**?

## Core Characteristics

What qualities repeatedly appear when Scripture discusses this theme?

## Biblical Vocabulary

Important Hebrew and Greek words associated with the subject.

```text

```

The final question is especially important:

> What does this particular passage contribute to my overall understanding of the theme?

Biblical people studies attempt to examine someone's entire biblical story rather than judging them from a single event.

Areas may include:

- Name

- Historical period
- Occupation
- Important relationships

- Strengths
- Weaknesses
- Faith
- Failures
- Character development
- Relationship with God
- Important passages
- Legacy
- Lessons from their life

I also try to distinguish between:

> **What Scripture describes**

and

> **What Scripture approves**

The Bible honestly records both righteous and sinful actions.

Simply because a biblical character does something does not necessarily mean Scripture endorses it.

# Hebrew and Greek Word Studies

Because the Old Testament was primarily written in Hebrew and the New Testament primarily in Greek, certain studies may examine important words in their original languages.

Word studies may include:

- Original Hebrew or Greek spelling
- Transliteration
- Pronunciation
- Definition
- Semantic range
- Other biblical occurrences
- How the word is used in the immediate passage
- How context affects its meaning

Word studies are intended to clarify Scripture rather than create meanings that are not present in the context.

A word's meaning is determined primarily by **how it is being used in the passage**, not simply by every possible dictionary definition.

### Important: Pulling After Making Your Own Changes

If you edit the same files that I later update, Git may need to combine both versions when you pull.

For example:

```text
You clone Genesis 1.md
        ↓
You add your own notes
        ↓
I update Genesis 1.md on GitHub
        ↓
You run git pull
```

If our changes affect different parts of the file, Git may combine them automatically.

If we both changed the same lines, Git may report a **merge conflict**.

This does not affect the original GitHub repository. The conflict only exists in your local copy and must be resolved before continuing.

# Study Principles

Several principles guide these studies.

## Context Matters

A verse should not be separated from:

```text
Verse
↓
Paragraph
↓
Chapter
↓
Book
↓
Historical Setting
↓
Entire Biblical Story
```

## Scripture Interprets Scripture

When possible, difficult passages should be compared with clearer passages addressing the same subject.

Cross-references are therefore an important part of these notes.

## Genre Matters

Different biblical genres should be interpreted appropriately.

The Bible contains:

- Narrative
- Law
- Poetry
- Wisdom literature
- Prophecy
- Gospel
- Letters
- Apocalyptic literature

Poetry should not always be interpreted like historical narrative.

Prophetic imagery should not always be interpreted like a straightforward command.

Understanding genre helps prevent misinterpretation.

## Historical Context Matters

The Bible was written within real historical settings.

Understanding:

- Ancient Near Eastern cultures
- Israelite history
- Assyrian rule
- Babylonian rule
- Persian rule
- Greek influence
- Roman rule
- Second Temple Judaism
- First-century Christianity

can often make difficult passages much clearer.

## Original Audience Matters

I try to first ask:

> What would the original audience have understood?

before asking:

> How does this apply to me?

## Application Matters

Bible study should not remain purely academic.

The purpose of understanding Scripture is ultimately connected to knowing God and living according to what Scripture teaches.
